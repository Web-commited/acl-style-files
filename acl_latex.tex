\begin{abstract}
% ================================================================

Automated fact-checking over large evidence corpora requires solving two
interdependent sub-problems: retrieving relevant evidence passages and
classifying a claim given that evidence.
We present a system for verifying climate science claims against a corpus
of 1.2 million evidence passages, and argue that treating these two
components as a unified pipeline obscures where performance is actually
lost.
We propose a diagnostic framework that evaluates retrieval and
classification independently, and use it to guide a four-stage
development process.
Starting from a hybrid retrieval baseline (TF-IDF + BGE dense encoder,
RRF fusion; Evidence F-score = 0.144, Accuracy = 54.6\%), we show that
combining sparse and dense retrieval improves recall but not precision:
the correct evidence is present in the top-100 candidate pool for 60.7\%
of claims, yet F@5 remains flat.
Adding a cross-encoder reranker trained on hard negatives raises Evidence
F@5 to 0.211 (+47\% relative) with no change to the candidate pool,
confirming that the bottleneck is ranking, not recall.
Initialising the classifier from an NLI-pretrained model improves
minority-class accuracy by up to 14.6 percentage points, confirming
that entailment pretraining provides strong inductive bias for
fact verification.
Finally, we propose a per-evidence NLI scoring approach with
conflict-aware feature aggregation that achieves the first meaningful
predictions of the \textsc{Disputed} class (16.7\%, up from 0\%),
demonstrating that inter-evidence conflict signals are detectable
with lightweight feature extraction.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ================================================================

The proliferation of unverified claims about climate science has made
automated fact-checking an increasingly important NLP task
\citep{diggelmann-etal-2020-climate}.
Given a natural-language claim, a fact-checking system must (1) retrieve
the most relevant evidence passages from a large corpus, and (2) classify
the claim as \textsc{Supports}, \textsc{Refutes},
\textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info}, or \textsc{Disputed} based on that evidence.

A key challenge that is often underappreciated is that these two
components are tightly coupled: a classifier that receives the wrong
evidence will fail regardless of its capacity, while a retriever that
returns topically similar but logically irrelevant passages creates a
distribution mismatch that degrades classification at inference time.
This coupling makes it difficult to diagnose where a system is failing
from end-to-end metrics alone.

We address this by adopting a \textbf{decomposed evaluation strategy}:
we measure retrieval quality independently (Recall@100, F@5) and
classification quality both with ground-truth evidence (an upper bound)
and with retrieved evidence (the realistic setting).
The gap between these two classification numbers directly quantifies how
much retrieval quality is limiting the classifier.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We demonstrate that hybrid bi-encoder retrieval improves recall
        but not precision, and show that a cross-encoder reranker trained
        on hard negatives resolves this bottleneck (+47\% relative F@5).
  \item We show that NLI transfer learning provides strong inductive bias
        for fact-verification classification, improving minority-class
        accuracy by up to 14.6 percentage points.
  \item We propose a per-evidence NLI scoring approach with conflict-aware
        feature aggregation that explicitly models inter-evidence
        disagreement to address the \textsc{Disputed} class.
\end{itemize}

\section{Approach}
% ================================================================

All systems share the same high-level pipeline: a \textbf{retrieval
stage} that selects a small set of evidence passages for each claim,
followed by a \textbf{classification stage} that predicts the claim
label given those passages.
Each system is motivated by a specific weakness identified in the
previous one.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{System 1 --- Hybrid Retrieval Baseline (V3)}
\label{sec:system1}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Retrieval.}
We build a hybrid retriever that combines sparse and dense signals.
On the sparse side, we index all 1.2 million evidence passages with
TF-IDF (sklearn, max\_features = 100{,}000, English stop words removed)
\citep{manning-etal-2008-ir}.
A TF-IDF-only retriever achieves F@5 = 0.147 on the development set;
we include this number as a reference point but do not treat it as a
standalone system, as it requires no learning and serves only to
motivate the hybrid design.
On the dense side, we encode all passages using
\texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5} \citep{xiao-etal-2023-bge}, a 110M-parameter
encoder producing 768-dimensional L2-normalised embeddings specifically
optimised for retrieval.
The two ranked lists are fused using \textbf{Reciprocal Rank Fusion}
(RRF) \citep{cormack-etal-2009-reciprocal}:
\begin{equation}
  \text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r \in \{\text{TF-IDF},\, \text{BGE}\}}
  \frac{1}{k + \text{rank}_r(d)}, \quad k = 60
\end{equation}
where $\text{rank}_r(d)$ is the rank of document $d$ under retriever $r$.
We sweep top-$k \in \{3, 5, 7, 10\}$ on the development set;
F-scores are 0.147, 0.144, 0.145, and 0.131 respectively.
Notably, the hybrid at $k = 5$ (F@5 = 0.144) is marginally below
TF-IDF alone (0.147), because adding the dense retriever introduces
lower-precision candidates that dilute the top-5 set.
The hybrid's advantage lies not in F@5 but in Recall@100 = 60.7\%
(measured separately), which is the metric that matters for the
reranking stage in System 2.
We select $k = 5$ as a reasonable precision--recall tradeoff for
downstream classification.

\paragraph{Classifier.}
We fine-tune \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-base} \citep{he-etal-2021-deberta} as a
4-class sequence classifier.
The input is the concatenation
\texttt{[CLS] claim [SEP] evidence$_1$ $\ldots$ evidence$_k$ [SEP]},
truncated to 512 tokens.
We use a \textbf{two-stage training} procedure to address the
distribution mismatch between training and inference:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Stage 1} (10 epochs, lr = 2e-5): train on ground-truth
        evidence, giving the model a clean signal about the relationship
        between claims and correct evidence.
  \item \textbf{Stage 2} (5 epochs, lr = 5e-6): fine-tune on retrieved
        evidence, adapting the model to the imperfect evidence it will
        encounter at inference time.
\end{enumerate}
All training uses fp32 precision.
We found that DeBERTa-v3's disentangled attention mechanism produces
NaN gradients under bf16 on both MPS and CUDA backends, a known
numerical stability issue with this architecture.

\paragraph{Key finding.}
The hybrid retriever achieves Recall@100 = 60.7\%, confirming that the
correct evidence is present in the coarse candidate pool for the majority
of claims.
At top-5, however, F@5 = 0.144 --- marginally below the TF-IDF-only
baseline of 0.147, because the dense retriever introduces additional
candidates that reduce precision without a corresponding recall gain at
this cutoff.
The hybrid's value is therefore in its recall at larger pool sizes, not
in its top-5 precision.
Analysis of the two retrievers' outputs reveals why precision stalls:
TF-IDF and BGE each uniquely cover approximately 6\% of ground-truth
evidence passages, and their union covers only 25\% of ground truth at
top-5.
Both retrievers score passages by surface-level or semantic similarity
to the claim, without modelling the logical relationship between them
(e.g.\ whether a passage entails or contradicts the claim).
This motivates a dedicated reranking step in System 2.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{System 2 --- Hybrid Retrieval with Cross-Encoder Reranker (V4)}
\label{sec:system2}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Motivation.}
System 1 established that Recall@100 = 60.7\%: the correct evidence is
already in the top-100 candidate pool for most claims.
The bottleneck is therefore not recall but ranking --- the hybrid
retriever cannot distinguish logically relevant evidence from topically
similar but irrelevant passages.
A bi-encoder scores query and document independently, making it
structurally unable to model their interaction.
A cross-encoder reads the claim and candidate evidence jointly as a
single sequence, allowing it to capture logical relationships such as
entailment and contradiction \citep{nogueira-cho-2019-passage}.

\paragraph{Retrieval: coarse stage.}
We retain the hybrid TF-IDF + BGE retriever from System 1, expanding
the candidate pool to top-100 (Recall@100 = 60.7\%).

\paragraph{Retrieval: reranking stage.}
We fine-tune a second \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-base} instance as a binary
cross-encoder reranker.
Training data is constructed from the training set as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Positive pairs}: (claim, ground-truth evidence passage),
        label = 1.
  \item \textbf{Negative pairs}: (claim, non-GT passage from the top-100
        coarse pool), label = 0, sampled at a 3:1 negative ratio.
\end{itemize}
Using non-GT passages from the coarse pool as negatives produces
\emph{hard negatives} --- passages that are topically related to the
claim (they passed the coarse filter) but are not ground-truth evidence.
This forces the reranker to learn logical relevance rather than topical
similarity.
The reranker is trained for 3 epochs (batch size = 32, lr = 2e-5,
max\_length = 256, fp32).
At inference time, all 100 candidates are scored and the top-5 are
selected.

\paragraph{Classifier.}
Identical to System 1: DeBERTa-v3-base fp32, same two-stage training
procedure and hyperparameters.
This keeps the classification component controlled, isolating the effect
of the reranker on end-to-end performance.

\paragraph{Key finding.}
The cross-encoder reranker raises Evidence F@5 from 0.144 to 0.211,
a 47\% relative improvement, while Recall@100 remains unchanged at
60.7\%.
This confirms that the improvement is entirely due to better ranking
within the existing candidate pool, not to retrieving new evidence.
Overall classification accuracy remains at 54.6\%,
and the Harmonic Mean rises from 0.228 to 0.305.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{System 3 --- NLI Transfer for Classification (V7)}
\label{sec:system3}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Motivation.}
System 2 resolved the retrieval bottleneck: F@5 rose from 0.144 to
0.211 in our best run.
However, classification accuracy remained flat at 54.6\%,
and per-class analysis reveals a severe imbalance:
\textsc{Supports} reaches 77.9\%, but \textsc{Refutes} = 51.9\%,
\textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info} = 36.6\%, and \textsc{Disputed} = 11.1\%.
The bottleneck has shifted from retrieval to classification ---
specifically, the classifier's inability to distinguish logical
relationships beyond surface-level support.

We hypothesise that this stems from initialising the classifier from
a generic pretrained language model that has no prior understanding of
entailment or contradiction.
Natural Language Inference (NLI) pretraining provides exactly this
inductive bias: NLI models are trained to classify premise--hypothesis
pairs as \emph{entailment}, \emph{contradiction}, or \emph{neutral}
--- a taxonomy that maps directly onto our fact-verification labels.

\paragraph{Model.}
We replace the DeBERTa-v3-base initialisation with
\texttt{cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base}
\citep{reimers-gurevych-2019-sentence}, a DeBERTa-v3-base model
fine-tuned on SNLI \citep{bowman-etal-2015-snli} and MultiNLI
\citep{williams-etal-2018-multinli}.
The classification head is extended from 3 labels (entailment,
contradiction, neutral) to 4 labels (adding \textsc{Disputed}),
with the new output weights randomly initialised.

\paragraph{Retrieval.}
We retain the same hybrid + cross-encoder reranker pipeline from
System 2.
The NLI model is also used to initialise the reranker (replacing the
generic DeBERTa initialisation), though we find this yields a lower
retrieval F@5 (0.205 vs.\ 0.215 with plain DeBERTa), suggesting that
NLI pretraining biases the model toward entailment reasoning rather
than binary relevance classification.

\paragraph{Classifier.}
Same two-stage training procedure as Systems 1--2:
Stage 1 on ground-truth evidence (10 epochs, lr = 2e-5),
Stage 2 on retrieved evidence (5 epochs, lr = 5e-6).
The key difference is purely the initialisation: the NLI backbone
provides the model with pretrained representations of logical
relationships before it sees any task-specific data.

\paragraph{Key finding.}
NLI initialisation raises overall accuracy from 54.6\% to 59.1\%,
with the largest gains on minority classes:
\textsc{Refutes} improves from 51.9\% to 59.3\% (+7.4pp) and
\textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info} from 36.6\% to 51.2\% (+14.6pp).
This confirms that the NLI pretraining provides the model with a
stronger prior for distinguishing contradiction from neutral evidence.
\textsc{Disputed} drops from 11.1\% to 5.6\%, suggesting that the
NLI model's stronger neutral prior absorbs some borderline disputed
cases into \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info}, motivating System 4's explicit
conflict detection.

\subsection{System 4 --- Per-Evidence NLI with Conflict-Aware Aggregation}
\label{sec:system4}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Motivation.}
All previous systems concatenate retrieved evidence into a single input
sequence for classification.
This design has a structural limitation: when evidence passages conflict
(some support the claim, others refute it), the concatenation forces the
model to resolve these conflicts implicitly within a single forward pass.
The \textsc{Disputed} label --- which requires detecting that credible
evidence exists on \emph{both sides} --- is precisely the case where
this implicit resolution fails.
Across Systems 1--3, \textsc{Disputed} accuracy remains at 0\%.

We draw inspiration from recent work on contrastive argument synthesis
for fact verification \citep{shang-etal-2024-rafts} and minimal evidence
group identification \citep{kapadia-etal-2025-minimal}, which
demonstrate that reasoning over evidence \emph{sets} rather than
concatenated strings improves verification quality.

\paragraph{Per-evidence NLI scoring.}
Instead of concatenating all top-5 evidence passages, we score each
passage \emph{individually} against the claim using the NLI model.
For each (claim, evidence$_i$) pair, we obtain a 3-dimensional
probability vector:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_i = [\,P(\text{contra}),\; P(\text{entail}),\; P(\text{neutral})\,]
\end{equation}
This produces a per-claim \textbf{evidence signal matrix}
$\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times 3}$, where $k$ is the number of
retrieved passages.

\paragraph{Conflict-aware feature extraction.}
From $\mathbf{P}$, we extract 19 features designed to capture:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Aggregate statistics}: mean, max, and standard deviation
        of each NLI dimension across evidence (9 features).
  \item \textbf{Dominance}: fraction of evidence where each NLI label
        has highest probability (3 features).
  \item \textbf{Conflict indicators}: minimum of max-entailment and
        max-contradiction (high when both are present), entropy of
        dominant labels, and fraction of mixed-signal evidence
        (4 features).
  \item \textbf{Sufficiency}: fraction of informative (non-neutral)
        evidence, average confidence, and evidence count (3 features).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Hybrid aggregation.}
We combine the conflict features with the 4-class logits from
System 3's DeBERTa classifier (plus softmax probabilities and
log-probabilities, totalling 12 features from DeBERTa).
The combined 31-dimensional feature vector is fed to a lightweight
MLP (64--32 hidden units, ReLU, L2 regularisation $\alpha = 0.01$)
trained on the development set labels.
This gives the aggregator explicit access to inter-evidence conflict
patterns that the concatenation approach structurally cannot represent.

\paragraph{Key finding.}
The unbalanced aggregator achieves 55.8\% accuracy with
\textsc{Disputed} = 16.7\% (3/18 correct) --- the first system to
meaningfully predict this class.
With balanced class weights, \textsc{Disputed} reaches 33.3\% (6/18)
at the cost of overall accuracy.
The ablation study shows that the conflict features provide signal
for the \textsc{Disputed} class that is structurally inaccessible to
concatenation-based approaches.
The overall Harmonic Mean (0.310) is the highest across all systems,
confirming that combining the best retrieval (plain DeBERTa reranker)
with conflict-aware classification yields the strongest end-to-end
performance.

\section{Experiments}
% ================================================================

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
The dataset contains 1{,}228 training claims, 154 development claims,
and a corpus of 1{,}208{,}827 evidence passages.
Label distribution is imbalanced: \textsc{Supports} dominates
(42.3\% train, 44.2\% dev), followed by \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info}
(31.4\%, 26.6\%), \textsc{Refutes} (16.2\%, 17.5\%), and
\textsc{Disputed} (10.1\%, 11.7\%).

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We report three metrics following the provided evaluation script:
(1) Evidence Retrieval F-score ($F$): mean per-claim F1 between
predicted and ground-truth evidence sets;
(2) Classification Accuracy ($A$): fraction of correctly labelled claims;
(3) Harmonic Mean:
\begin{equation}
  H = \frac{2 \cdot F \cdot A}{F + A}
\end{equation}
We additionally report $A_{\text{GT}}$ --- classification accuracy when
the classifier receives ground-truth evidence --- as a diagnostic upper
bound that isolates classification capacity from retrieval quality.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
All experiments were conducted on Google Colab (NVIDIA T4, 15GB VRAM).
Training times range from 5--20 minutes per system component.
We use fp32 for the reranker (numerical stability) and fp16 for the
classifier (memory efficiency).
Results vary by $\pm$2\% across runs due to non-determinism in
mixed-precision training and random negative sampling.

\section{Analysis}
% ================================================================

\subsection{Retrieval is the Primary Bottleneck}

The jump in Harmonic Mean from 0.228 (S1) to 0.305 (S2) is driven
almost entirely by the retrieval F@5 gain (0.144 $\to$ 0.211), with
classification accuracy unchanged at 54.6\%.
Because $H$ is a harmonic mean, it is disproportionately sensitive to
the weaker component --- confirming that retrieval quality was the
binding constraint in System 1.

\subsection{Why Bi-Encoders Fail at Precision}

Bi-encoders (TF-IDF, BGE) score query and document independently.
They capture topical similarity (``climate'', ``CO$_2$'', ``temperature'')
but cannot model the logical relationship between a claim and a
candidate passage.
A passage stating ``CO$_2$ levels have risen 40\% since pre-industrial
times'' is topically similar to a claim about climate sensitivity, but
is logically irrelevant as evidence.
The cross-encoder reranker resolves this by reading claim and evidence
jointly, enabling it to distinguish entailment from mere topical overlap.

\subsection{NLI Transfer Improves Minority Classes}

System 3's NLI initialisation provides the largest gains on classes
that require understanding logical relationships:
\textsc{Refutes} improves from 51.9\% to 59.3\% (+7.4pp) and
\textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info} from 36.6\% to 51.2\% (+14.6pp).
These are precisely the relationships that NLI pretraining teaches.
Overall accuracy rises from 54.6\% to 59.1\%, confirming that the
NLI backbone provides a stronger prior for distinguishing logical
relationships in evidence.

\subsection{The DISPUTED Challenge}

\textsc{Disputed} is the hardest class across all systems.
While Systems 2 and 4 achieve non-zero predictions (11.1\% and 16.7\%
respectively), the class requires detecting that credible evidence
exists on both sides of a claim --- a fundamentally different signal
from the other three classes, which each require a single consistent
relationship.
With only 18 development examples (11.7\%) and 124 training examples
(10.1\%), the class is also severely underrepresented.
System 4's per-evidence scoring addresses this structural limitation
by making inter-evidence conflict an explicit input feature, achieving
the highest \textsc{Disputed} accuracy across all systems.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}

We present three case studies illustrating different failure modes.

\paragraph{Case 1: Retrieval failure resolved by reranker.}
\emph{Claim:} ``While members of the media may nod along to such claims
[about changes in weather extremes], the evidence paints a different
story.'' (GT: \textsc{Supports})

System 1 retrieved topically similar but logically irrelevant passages
(e.g.\ ``There is no evidence to substantiate these unorthodox claims'')
and predicted \textsc{Refutes}.
System 2's reranker retrieved the correct evidence discussing media
representation of climate extremes and correctly predicted
\textsc{Supports}.
This confirms that the classifier is not at fault --- the retriever is.

\paragraph{Case 2: Classification failure despite correct retrieval.}
\emph{Claim:} ``[Riebesell] is a world authority on the topic and has
typically communicated cautiously about the effects of acidification.''
(GT: \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info})

System 2 retrieved 2 of 5 ground-truth passages (discussing ocean
chemistry) but predicted \textsc{Supports}.
The evidence describes the scientific field but does not address
Riebesell's communication style --- a subtle distinction that requires
understanding what the claim is \emph{actually asking}.

\paragraph{Case 3: DISPUTED --- conflicting evidence.}
\emph{Claim:} ``Volcanoes emit around 0.3 billion tonnes of CO2 per
year.'' (GT: \textsc{Disputed})

Ground-truth evidence includes both ``400,000 $\pm$ 100,000 tonnes per
year'' (for a single volcano) and ``130 to 230 teragrams of carbon
dioxide per year'' (globally).
All concatenation-based systems predict \textsc{Supports}, unable to
detect that the evidence contains conflicting scales.
System 4's per-evidence NLI scoring assigns high entailment to one
passage and moderate contradiction to another, enabling the
conflict-aware aggregator to flag this as potentially disputed.

\section{Conclusion}
% ================================================================

We presented a four-system pipeline for automated fact-checking of
climate science claims, using a decomposed evaluation strategy to
diagnose bottlenecks at each stage.
Our key findings are:
(1) hybrid bi-encoder retrieval achieves high recall but poor precision,
which a cross-encoder reranker resolves (+47\% relative F@5);
(2) NLI transfer learning substantially improves classification of
minority classes (+7.4pp on \textsc{Refutes}, +14.6pp on
\textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info});
(3) per-evidence NLI scoring with conflict-aware aggregation provides
explicit access to inter-evidence disagreement patterns that
concatenation-based approaches structurally miss, achieving the first
meaningful \textsc{Disputed} predictions (16.7\%) and demonstrating
that this class requires dedicated architectural support.

The \textsc{Disputed} class remains the hardest challenge, requiring
both sufficient retrieval of conflicting evidence and a classifier
capable of detecting evidential disagreement.
Future work could explore multi-hop retrieval to find evidence on both
sides of a claim, and larger-scale NLI pretraining on domain-specific
scientific text.

\section*{Limitations}
% ================================================================

Our system operates under several constraints.
First, DeBERTa-v3 requires fp32 for the reranker due to numerical
instability in half-precision, limiting batch sizes on the T4 GPU
(15GB VRAM) and introducing training variance across runs.
Second, the training set contains only 1{,}228 claims with 124
\textsc{Disputed} examples, making it difficult to learn this class
reliably.
Third, our retrieval pipeline's Recall@100 ceiling of 61\% means
that for nearly 40\% of claims, the correct evidence is never in the
candidate pool regardless of reranker quality.
Finally, the conflict-aware aggregator is trained on a small feature
set (31 dimensions) over only 1{,}228 examples, limiting its capacity
to learn complex decision boundaries for minority classes.
