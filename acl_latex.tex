\begin{abstract}
% ================================================================

Automated fact-checking over large evidence corpora requires solving two
interdependent sub-problems: retrieving relevant evidence passages and
classifying a claim given that evidence.
We present a system for verifying climate science claims against a corpus
of 1.2 million evidence passages, and argue that treating these two
components as a unified pipeline obscures where performance is actually
lost.
We propose a diagnostic framework that evaluates retrieval and
classification independently, and use it to guide a four-stage
development process.
Starting from a hybrid retrieval baseline (TF-IDF + BGE dense encoder,
RRF fusion; Evidence F-score = 0.144, Accuracy = 54.6\%), we show that
combining sparse and dense retrieval improves recall but not precision:
the correct evidence is present in the top-100 candidate pool for 60.7\%
of claims, yet F@5 remains flat.
Adding a cross-encoder reranker trained on hard negatives raises Evidence
F@5 to 0.238 (+65\% relative) with no change to the candidate pool,
confirming that the bottleneck is ranking, not recall.

\section{Introduction}
% ================================================================

The proliferation of unverified claims about climate science has made
automated fact-checking an increasingly important NLP task
\citep{diggelmann-etal-2020-climate}.
Given a natural-language claim, a fact-checking system must (1) retrieve
the most relevant evidence passages from a large corpus, and (2) classify
the claim as \textsc{Supports}, \textsc{Refutes},
\textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info}, or \textsc{Disputed} based on that evidence.

A key challenge that is often underappreciated is that these two
components are tightly coupled: a classifier that receives the wrong
evidence will fail regardless of its capacity, while a retriever that
returns topically similar but logically irrelevant passages creates a
distribution mismatch that degrades classification at inference time.
This coupling makes it difficult to diagnose where a system is failing
from end-to-end metrics alone.

We address this by adopting a \textbf{decomposed evaluation strategy}:
we measure retrieval quality independently (Recall@100, F@5) and
classification quality both with ground-truth evidence (an upper bound)
and with retrieved evidence (the realistic setting).
The gap between these two classification numbers directly quantifies how
much retrieval quality is limiting the classifier.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We demonstrate that hybrid bi-encoder retrieval improves recall
        but not precision, and show that a cross-encoder reranker trained
        on hard negatives resolves this bottleneck (+65\% relative F@5).

\section{Approach}
% ================================================================

All systems share the same high-level pipeline: a \textbf{retrieval
stage} that selects a small set of evidence passages for each claim,
followed by a \textbf{classification stage} that predicts the claim
label given those passages.
We describe two fully implemented systems here; Systems 3 and 4 are
documented separately by other team members.
Each system is motivated by a specific weakness identified in the
previous one.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{System 1 --- Hybrid Retrieval Baseline (V3)}
\label{sec:system1}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Retrieval.}
We build a hybrid retriever that combines sparse and dense signals.
On the sparse side, we index all 1.2 million evidence passages with
TF-IDF (sklearn, max\_features = 100{,}000, English stop words removed)
\citep{manning-etal-2008-ir}.
A TF-IDF-only retriever achieves F@5 = 0.147 on the development set;
we include this number as a reference point but do not treat it as a
standalone system, as it requires no learning and serves only to
motivate the hybrid design.
On the dense side, we encode all passages using
\texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5} \citep{xiao-etal-2023-bge}, a 110M-parameter
encoder producing 768-dimensional L2-normalised embeddings specifically
optimised for retrieval.
The two ranked lists are fused using \textbf{Reciprocal Rank Fusion}
(RRF) \citep{cormack-etal-2009-reciprocal}:
\begin{equation}
  \text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r \in \{\text{TF-IDF},\, \text{BGE}\}}
  \frac{1}{k + \text{rank}_r(d)}, \quad k = 60
\end{equation}
where $\text{rank}_r(d)$ is the rank of document $d$ under retriever $r$.
We sweep top-$k \in \{3, 5, 7, 10\}$ on the development set;
F-scores are 0.147, 0.144, 0.145, and 0.131 respectively, so we select
$k = 5$ as the operating point.

\paragraph{Classifier.}
We fine-tune \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-base} \citep{he-etal-2021-deberta} as a
4-class sequence classifier.
The input is the concatenation
\texttt{[CLS] claim [SEP] evidence$_1$ $\ldots$ evidence$_k$ [SEP]},
truncated to 512 tokens.
We use a \textbf{two-stage training} procedure to address the
distribution mismatch between training and inference:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Stage 1} (10 epochs, lr = 2e-5): train on ground-truth
        evidence, giving the model a clean signal about the relationship
        between claims and correct evidence.
  \item \textbf{Stage 2} (5 epochs, lr = 5e-6): fine-tune on retrieved
        evidence, adapting the model to the imperfect evidence it will
        encounter at inference time.
\end{enumerate}
All training uses fp32 precision.
We found that DeBERTa-v3's disentangled attention mechanism produces
NaN gradients under bf16 on both MPS and CUDA backends, a known
numerical stability issue with this architecture.

\paragraph{Key finding.}
The hybrid retriever achieves Recall@100 = 60.7\%, confirming that the
correct evidence is present in the coarse candidate pool for the majority
of claims.
However, F@5 = 0.144 --- barely above the TF-IDF-only baseline of 0.147.
Analysis of the two retrievers' outputs reveals why: TF-IDF and BGE each
uniquely cover approximately 6\% of ground-truth evidence passages, and
their union covers only 25\% of ground truth at top-5.
The retrievers are complementary in recall but both fail at the same
precision problem: they score passages by surface-level or semantic
similarity to the claim, without modelling the logical relationship
between them (e.g.\ whether a passage entails or contradicts the claim).
This motivates a dedicated reranking step in System 2.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{System 2 --- Hybrid Retrieval with Cross-Encoder Reranker (V4)}
\label{sec:system2}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Motivation.}
System 1 established that Recall@100 = 60.7\%: the correct evidence is
already in the top-100 candidate pool for most claims.
The bottleneck is therefore not recall but ranking --- the hybrid
retriever cannot distinguish logically relevant evidence from topically
similar but irrelevant passages.
A bi-encoder scores query and document independently, making it
structurally unable to model their interaction.
A cross-encoder reads the claim and candidate evidence jointly as a
single sequence, allowing it to capture logical relationships such as
entailment and contradiction \citep{nogueira-cho-2019-passage}.

\paragraph{Retrieval: coarse stage.}
We retain the hybrid TF-IDF + BGE retriever from System 1, expanding
the candidate pool to top-100 (Recall@100 = 60.7\%).

\paragraph{Retrieval: reranking stage.}
We fine-tune a second \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-base} instance as a binary
cross-encoder reranker.
Training data is constructed from the training set as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Positive pairs}: (claim, ground-truth evidence passage),
        label = 1.
  \item \textbf{Negative pairs}: (claim, non-GT passage from the top-100
        coarse pool), label = 0, sampled at a 3:1 negative ratio.
\end{itemize}
Using non-GT passages from the coarse pool as negatives produces
\emph{hard negatives} --- passages that are topically related to the
claim (they passed the coarse filter) but are not ground-truth evidence.
This forces the reranker to learn logical relevance rather than topical
similarity.
The reranker is trained for 3 epochs (batch size = 32, lr = 2e-5,
max\_length = 256, fp32).
At inference time, all 100 candidates are scored and the top-5 are
selected.

\paragraph{Classifier.}
Identical to System 1: DeBERTa-v3-base fp32, same two-stage training
procedure and hyperparameters.
This keeps the classification component controlled, isolating the effect
of the reranker on end-to-end performance.

\paragraph{Key finding.}
The cross-encoder reranker raises Evidence F@5 from 0.144 to 0.238,
a 65\% relative improvement, while Recall@100 remains unchanged at
60.7\%.
This confirms that the improvement is entirely due to better ranking
within the existing candidate pool, not to retrieving new evidence.
Overall classification accuracy improves from 54.6\% to 56.5\%,
and the Harmonic Mean rises from 0.228 to 0.334.
