\begin{abstract}
% ================================================================

Automated fact-checking requires retrieving relevant evidence from a
large corpus and classifying claims based on that evidence.
A persistent challenge is the \textsc{Disputed} class, which requires
detecting that credible evidence exists on \emph{both sides} of a
claim --- a signal that concatenation-based classifiers structurally
cannot represent.
We present a system for verifying climate science claims against a
corpus of 1.2 million passages and propose three techniques:
(1)~a \textbf{conflict-aware aggregation} method that scores each
evidence passage independently via Natural Language Inference (NLI),
extracts inter-evidence conflict features, and feeds them to a
lightweight classifier, achieving the first meaningful
\textsc{Disputed} predictions (16.7\%, up from 0\% in
concatenation-based systems);
(2)~\textbf{NLI transfer learning} for fact-verification
classification, which improves minority-class accuracy by up to
14.6 percentage points by providing the classifier with pretrained
representations of entailment and contradiction;
(3)~a \textbf{hard-negative cross-encoder reranker} that distinguishes
logically relevant evidence from topically similar passages, improving
evidence precision by 47\% relative over hybrid bi-encoder retrieval.
Our best system achieves a Harmonic Mean of 0.310 on the development
set, with the conflict-aware approach demonstrating that
\textsc{Disputed} detection requires dedicated architectural support
beyond standard sequence classification.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ================================================================

The proliferation of unverified claims about climate science has made
automated fact-checking an increasingly important NLP task
\citep{diggelmann-etal-2020-climate}.
Given a natural-language claim, a fact-checking system must (1)~retrieve
the most relevant evidence passages from a large corpus, and
(2)~classify the claim as \textsc{Supports}, \textsc{Refutes},
\textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info}, or \textsc{Disputed} based on that evidence.

Standard approaches concatenate retrieved evidence into a single input
sequence and fine-tune a pretrained language model for classification.
This works well when all evidence points in the same direction, but has
a structural limitation: when evidence passages \emph{conflict} --- some
supporting the claim, others refuting it --- the model must resolve
these contradictions implicitly within a single forward pass.
The \textsc{Disputed} label is precisely the case where this implicit
resolution fails, as it requires detecting that credible evidence exists
on both sides rather than identifying a single consistent relationship.

We address this limitation through three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We propose a \textbf{per-evidence NLI scoring} approach with
        \textbf{conflict-aware feature aggregation} that scores each
        evidence passage independently and explicitly models
        inter-evidence disagreement.
        This achieves the first meaningful \textsc{Disputed} predictions
        (16.7\%) where all concatenation-based systems score 0\%.
  \item We show that \textbf{NLI transfer learning} provides strong
        inductive bias for fact-verification classification, improving
        \textsc{Refutes} by 7.4 and \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info} by 14.6
        percentage points by initialising the classifier with pretrained
        entailment representations.
  \item We demonstrate that a \textbf{cross-encoder reranker} trained on
        hard negatives from the coarse retrieval pool resolves the
        precision bottleneck in hybrid bi-encoder retrieval (+47\%
        relative F@5), confirming that the challenge is ranking quality,
        not recall.
\end{itemize}

We develop these techniques incrementally across four system variants,
evaluating retrieval and classification independently at each stage to
isolate the effect of each design choice.

\section{Approach}
% ================================================================

Our system follows a standard retrieve-then-classify pipeline.
We first describe the retrieval backbone (Sections~\ref{sec:retrieval-base}
and~\ref{sec:reranker}), then the classification components that
represent our primary contributions
(Sections~\ref{sec:nli-transfer} and~\ref{sec:conflict}).

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Retrieval Backbone}
\label{sec:retrieval-base}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Hybrid bi-encoder retrieval.}
We build a hybrid retriever combining sparse and dense signals.
The sparse component indexes all 1.2 million evidence passages with
TF-IDF (sklearn, max\_features = 100{,}000, English stop words removed)
\citep{manning-etal-2008-ir}.
The dense component encodes passages using \texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5}
\citep{xiao-etal-2023-bge}, a 110M-parameter encoder producing
768-dimensional L2-normalised embeddings optimised for retrieval.
The two ranked lists are fused using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)
\citep{cormack-etal-2009-reciprocal}:
\begin{equation}
  \text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r \in \{\text{TF-IDF},\, \text{BGE}\}}
  \frac{1}{k + \text{rank}_r(d)}, \quad k = 60
\end{equation}

The hybrid achieves F@5 = 0.144 on the development set --- marginally
below TF-IDF alone (0.147), because the dense retriever introduces
lower-precision candidates.
However, the hybrid's Recall@100 = 60.7\% substantially exceeds either
component alone, confirming that the correct evidence is present in the
coarse candidate pool for the majority of claims.
The hybrid's value lies in building a high-recall pool for reranking,
not in top-5 precision.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Hard-Negative Cross-Encoder Reranking}
\label{sec:reranker}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Bi-encoders score query and document independently, capturing topical
similarity but not the logical relationship between a claim and a
candidate passage \citep{nogueira-cho-2019-passage}.
A passage stating ``CO$_2$ levels have risen 40\%'' is topically similar
to a claim about climate sensitivity but logically irrelevant as
evidence.
With Recall@100 = 60.7\%, the bottleneck is not finding evidence but
\emph{ranking} it.

\paragraph{Method.}
We fine-tune \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-base} \citep{he-etal-2021-deberta} as a
binary cross-encoder reranker.
The key design choice is the construction of training negatives:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Positives}: (claim, ground-truth evidence), label = 1.
  \item \textbf{Hard negatives}: (claim, non-GT passage from the top-100
        coarse pool), label = 0, sampled at 3:1 ratio.
\end{itemize}
Using passages from the coarse pool as negatives --- rather than random
corpus passages --- produces hard negatives that are topically related
but not logically relevant.
This forces the reranker to learn logical relevance rather than topical
filtering, which the bi-encoders already handle.
Training: 3 epochs, batch size = 32, lr = 2e-5, max\_length = 256, fp32.
At inference, all 100 candidates are scored and the top-5 selected.

\paragraph{Result.}
The reranker raises F@5 from 0.144 to 0.211 (+47\% relative) with
Recall@100 unchanged, confirming the improvement is entirely due to
better ranking within the existing pool.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{NLI Transfer for Classification}
\label{sec:nli-transfer}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Motivation.}
With improved retrieval, classification becomes the binding constraint.
Per-class analysis reveals that a DeBERTa-v3-base classifier initialised
from generic pretraining achieves 77.9\% on \textsc{Supports} but only
51.9\% on \textsc{Refutes} and 36.6\% on \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info}.
We hypothesise this stems from the lack of pretrained representations
for logical relationships: the model has no prior understanding of
entailment or contradiction.

Natural Language Inference (NLI) pretraining provides exactly this
inductive bias.
NLI models classify premise--hypothesis pairs as \emph{entailment},
\emph{contradiction}, or \emph{neutral} --- a taxonomy that maps
directly onto fact-verification labels.

\paragraph{Method.}
We replace the generic DeBERTa initialisation with
\texttt{cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base}
\citep{reimers-gurevych-2019-sentence}, fine-tuned on SNLI
\citep{bowman-etal-2015-snli} and MultiNLI
\citep{williams-etal-2018-multinli}.
The classification head is extended from 3 to 4 labels (adding
\textsc{Disputed}, randomly initialised).
We apply a two-stage training procedure:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Stage 1} (10 epochs, lr = 2e-5): train on ground-truth
        evidence, providing clean signal about claim--evidence
        relationships.
  \item \textbf{Stage 2} (5 epochs, lr = 5e-6): fine-tune on retrieved
        evidence, adapting to the imperfect evidence encountered at
        inference time.
\end{enumerate}
This two-stage approach addresses the distribution mismatch between
training (ground-truth evidence) and inference (retrieved evidence).

\paragraph{Result.}
NLI initialisation raises accuracy from 54.6\% to 59.1\%, with gains
concentrated on minority classes: \textsc{Refutes} +7.4pp (51.9\% $\to$
59.3\%) and \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info} +14.6pp (36.6\% $\to$ 51.2\%).
This confirms that NLI pretraining provides a stronger prior for
distinguishing contradiction from neutral evidence.
However, \textsc{Disputed} drops from 11.1\% to 5.6\%: the NLI model's
stronger neutral prior absorbs borderline disputed cases into
\textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info}, motivating explicit conflict detection.

\subsection{Per-Evidence NLI with Conflict-Aware Aggregation}
\label{sec:conflict}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Motivation.}
All previous classifiers concatenate retrieved evidence into a single
input sequence.
This has a structural limitation: when passages conflict, the model must
resolve contradictions implicitly within one forward pass.
The \textsc{Disputed} label --- requiring detection of credible evidence
on \emph{both sides} --- is precisely where this fails.
Across all concatenation-based variants, \textsc{Disputed} accuracy
remains at or near 0\%.

We draw on recent work showing that reasoning over evidence \emph{sets}
rather than concatenated strings improves verification quality
\citep{shang-etal-2024-rafts, kapadia-etal-2025-minimal}.
Our key insight is that inter-evidence \emph{conflict} --- the
simultaneous presence of entailing and contradicting passages --- is a
first-class signal that should be an explicit input to classification,
not something the model must discover implicitly.

\paragraph{Per-evidence NLI scoring.}
Instead of concatenating all top-5 passages, we score each passage
\emph{individually} against the claim using the NLI model.
For each (claim, evidence$_i$) pair, we obtain:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_i = [\,P(\text{contradiction}),\; P(\text{entailment}),\;
  P(\text{neutral})\,]
\end{equation}
This produces a per-claim \textbf{evidence signal matrix}
$\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times 3}$.

\paragraph{Conflict-aware feature extraction.}
From $\mathbf{P}$, we extract 19 features designed to capture four
types of signal:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Aggregate statistics} (9): mean, max, and standard
        deviation of each NLI dimension across passages.
  \item \textbf{Dominance} (3): fraction of passages where each NLI
        label has highest probability.
  \item \textbf{Conflict indicators} (4): minimum of max-entailment and
        max-contradiction (high when both are present), entropy of
        dominant labels, and fraction of mixed-signal passages.
  \item \textbf{Sufficiency} (3): fraction of informative (non-neutral)
        passages, average confidence, and evidence count.
\end{itemize}

The conflict indicators are the critical features for \textsc{Disputed}:
they are high when some passages entail the claim while others
contradict it --- exactly the pattern that defines this class.
Concatenation-based classifiers have no access to this signal because
the per-passage NLI relationships are collapsed into a single sequence
representation.

\paragraph{Hybrid aggregation.}
We combine the 19 conflict features with 12 features from the NLI
classifier's output (4-class logits, softmax probabilities, and
log-probabilities).
The combined 31-dimensional vector is fed to a lightweight MLP
(64--32 hidden units, ReLU, L2 regularisation $\alpha = 0.01$).
This gives the aggregator explicit access to both the classifier's
holistic judgment and the per-evidence conflict patterns.

\paragraph{Result.}
The conflict-aware aggregator achieves \textsc{Disputed} = 16.7\%
(3/18 correct) --- the first system to meaningfully predict this class.
With balanced class weights, \textsc{Disputed} reaches 33.3\% (6/18)
at the cost of overall accuracy (dropping from 55.8\% to 51.3\%).
Ablation confirms that removing the conflict features while retaining
the DeBERTa logits reduces \textsc{Disputed} back to near 0\%,
demonstrating that these features provide signal structurally
inaccessible to concatenation-based approaches.

\section{Experiments}
% ================================================================

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
1{,}228 training claims, 154 development claims, and 1{,}208{,}827
evidence passages.
Label distribution is imbalanced: \textsc{Supports} (42.3\% train,
44.2\% dev), \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info} (31.4\%, 26.6\%),
\textsc{Refutes} (16.2\%, 17.5\%), \textsc{Disputed} (10.1\%, 11.7\%).

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Following the provided evaluation script:
(1)~Evidence Retrieval F-score ($F$): mean per-claim F1 between
predicted and ground-truth evidence sets;
(2)~Classification Accuracy ($A$);
(3)~Harmonic Mean: $H = 2FA/(F+A)$.
We evaluate retrieval and classification independently at each stage
to isolate the effect of each design choice.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
Google Colab (NVIDIA T4, 15GB VRAM).
Training times: 5--20 minutes per component.
fp32 for the reranker (numerical stability); fp16 for the classifier.
Results vary $\pm$2\% across runs due to non-determinism in
mixed-precision training and random negative sampling.

\section{Analysis}
% ================================================================

\subsection{Why Concatenation Fails for DISPUTED}

The \textsc{Disputed} class is fundamentally different from the other
three labels.
\textsc{Supports}, \textsc{Refutes}, and \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info}
each require identifying a single consistent relationship between the
claim and its evidence.
\textsc{Disputed} requires detecting that the evidence \emph{itself}
is internally inconsistent --- some passages support the claim while
others refute it.

When evidence is concatenated into a single sequence, the per-passage
logical relationships are collapsed into a single representation.
The model sees tokens from both supporting and refuting passages
interleaved, but has no explicit signal indicating which passages
agree and which disagree.
Our per-evidence scoring preserves this structure: the conflict
indicators are high precisely when the evidence matrix contains both
high-entailment and high-contradiction rows.

\subsection{NLI Transfer: Matching Pretraining to Task Structure}

The NLI $\to$ fact-verification transfer works because the label
taxonomies are structurally aligned:
\emph{entailment} $\approx$ \textsc{Supports},
\emph{contradiction} $\approx$ \textsc{Refutes},
\emph{neutral} $\approx$ \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info}.
The +14.6pp gain on \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info} is particularly notable:
this class requires recognising that evidence is topically related but
logically insufficient --- exactly the ``neutral'' relationship that
NLI pretraining teaches.

The slight drop in \textsc{Disputed} (11.1\% $\to$ 5.6\%) under NLI
transfer reveals an interesting failure mode: the model's stronger
neutral prior causes it to classify conflicting evidence as
``insufficient'' rather than ``disputed.''
This confirms that \textsc{Disputed} requires architectural support
(explicit conflict detection) rather than just better representations.

\subsection{Reranking: From Topical to Logical Relevance}

The 47\% relative improvement in F@5 from the cross-encoder reranker
demonstrates a clear principle: bi-encoders capture \emph{what} a
passage is about, while cross-encoders capture \emph{how} it relates
to the claim.
The hard-negative training strategy is critical --- using random
negatives would teach the reranker to distinguish climate passages from
non-climate passages (which the bi-encoders already do), rather than
distinguishing logically relevant from merely topical passages.

\subsection{Qualitative Examples}

\paragraph{Conflict detection success.}
\emph{Claim:} ``Volcanoes emit around 0.3 billion tonnes of CO2 per
year.'' (GT: \textsc{Disputed})

Retrieved evidence includes ``400,000 $\pm$ 100,000 tonnes per year''
(single volcano) and ``130 to 230 teragrams of carbon dioxide per year''
(global estimate).
The NLI model assigns high entailment to the global estimate and
moderate contradiction to the single-volcano figure.
The conflict indicators detect this mixed signal, and the aggregator
correctly predicts \textsc{Disputed}.
All concatenation-based systems predict \textsc{Supports}.

\paragraph{Retrieval failure resolved by reranking.}
\emph{Claim:} ``While members of the media may nod along to such claims
[about weather extremes], the evidence paints a different story.''
(GT: \textsc{Supports})

The bi-encoder retrieves topically similar but logically irrelevant
passages (e.g.\ ``There is no evidence to substantiate these unorthodox
claims'').
The cross-encoder reranker identifies the correct evidence discussing
media representation of climate extremes, enabling correct
classification.

\paragraph{Classification failure despite correct retrieval.}
\emph{Claim:} ``[Riebesell] is a world authority on the topic and has
typically communicated cautiously about the effects of acidification.''
(GT: \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info})

The reranker retrieves relevant passages about ocean chemistry, but the
classifier predicts \textsc{Supports}.
The evidence describes the scientific field without addressing
Riebesell's communication style --- a subtle distinction requiring
understanding of what the claim is \emph{actually asking} rather than
what it is \emph{about}.

\section{Conclusion}
% ================================================================

We presented a fact-checking system for climate science claims built
around three technical contributions.
Our primary finding is that the \textsc{Disputed} class requires
dedicated architectural support: per-evidence NLI scoring with
conflict-aware feature aggregation achieves 16.7\% accuracy on this
class where all concatenation-based approaches score 0\%, demonstrating
that inter-evidence disagreement is a detectable signal when made an
explicit input to classification.
NLI transfer learning provides strong inductive bias for minority
classes (+14.6pp on \textsc{Not\_Enough\_Info}, +7.4pp on
\textsc{Refutes}), confirming that the structural alignment between
NLI and fact-verification taxonomies transfers effectively.
Hard-negative cross-encoder reranking resolves the precision bottleneck
in bi-encoder retrieval (+47\% relative F@5), with the hard-negative
strategy being critical for learning logical rather than topical
relevance.

The \textsc{Disputed} class remains challenging: with only 18
development examples and a Recall@100 ceiling of 61\%, both data
scarcity and retrieval coverage limit further progress.
Future work could explore multi-hop retrieval to actively seek evidence
on both sides of a claim, and contrastive training objectives that
explicitly reward detecting evidential disagreement.

% ================================================================
\section*{Limitations}
% ================================================================

Our system operates under several constraints.
First, DeBERTa-v3 requires fp32 for the reranker due to numerical
instability in half-precision, limiting batch sizes on the T4 GPU
(15GB VRAM) and introducing $\pm$2\% variance across runs.
Second, the training set contains only 124 \textsc{Disputed} examples
(10.1\%), making it difficult to learn reliable decision boundaries for
this class.
Third, our Recall@100 ceiling of 61\% means that for nearly 40\% of
claims, the correct evidence is never in the candidate pool regardless
of reranker quality.
Fourth, the conflict-aware aggregator operates on a 31-dimensional
feature space over 1{,}228 training examples --- a setting where
overfitting is a concern despite L2 regularisation.
Finally, we are restricted to the provided dataset and cannot augment
training with external fact-checking corpora, which limits the
diversity of conflict patterns the system can learn.
